**Method.** A conceptual argument from extended hands-on use of GPT-3, not
an experiment. It sets out to name an object that existing alignment
vocabulary misdescribes. "GPT" means transformers trained on next-token
prediction, and "simulator" means any model trained with predictive loss on a
self-supervised dataset, whatever the architecture or data type. The whole
frame holds for GPT only "insofar as it is inner-aligned" (Summary).

**Inadequate ontologies.** Five categories are tried and found wanting.
*Agent*: in the agent ontology the policy and the effective agent coincide,
and for GPT they do not. GPT-driven agents are ephemeral, can run in
parallel, and are not what the network wants. Calling GPT an agent that
wants to roleplay adds an unevidenced roleplayer. *Oracle*: GPT is trained
to be realistic rather than correct, so benchmarks built on supervised
question–answer assumptions underestimate it and miss its open-ended time
dimension. *Tool* and *genie*: tool-likeness and instruction following are
contingent, exploitable capabilities, orthogonal to its motive. *Behavior
cloning*: GPT clones the behavior of a universe, not one demonstrator,
compressed into a generative rule that can animate counterfactuals.

**The scheme.** The **simulator** is the learned rule, and the **simulacra**
are what it propagates: characters, agents, non-agentic processes and text
instances. The **simulation objective** is the outer objective of
self-supervised learning, Bayes-optimal conditional inference over the prior
of the training distribution. In its strict form it applies only to models
trained with a proper scoring rule, which excludes GANs. Its pressure
judges each action directly rather than by consequences, so it does not by
itself incentivize instrumental convergence.
The **prediction orthogonality thesis** says a model whose objective is
prediction can simulate agents with any objectives, at any optimality
bounded above but not below by the model's power. A footnote conditions it
on inner alignment. The physics analogy carries the frame: the simulator is
to its output as physics is to a person taking a test, or the Game of Life
rules are to a glider. Knowledge revealed by a test is a property of the
configuration, so global conclusions from prompt-contingent behavior commit a
fallacy of composition. The appendix table scores ten simulator-like systems
on six properties: self-supervised, converges to simulation objective,
generates rollouts, simulator/simulacra nonidentity, stochastic, evidential.
GPT and a model-based RL transition function have all six.

**Conclusions and hedges.** The post claims a better frame, not a finding.
Its reasons for the word "simulator" are
offered as after-the-fact rationalizations. It makes no claim about internal
mechanism. It lists its own open caveats: underdetermined conditions, biased
training samples, and whether a simulator trained on an agent's data
converges with the RL archetype. Among its next steps it leaves open how
RLHF, rejection sampling and STaR make models diverge from the simulation
objective, and notes that without inner alignment, prediction orthogonality
may fail.
