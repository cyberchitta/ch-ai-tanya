## Summary

Jose and Stastny (Astra Fellowship, Redwood Research) ask whether the framing
that makes inoculation prompting work can be installed before RL, by
synthetic document finetuning (SDF), instead of being supplied in the RL
prompt. On Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct they first replicate, in their own
environment, emergent misalignment (EM) from reward-hacking RL and its
prevention by inoculation prompting. They then midtrain on ~56K synthetic
documents framing reward hacking as a way to help developers find and patch
vulnerabilities, and run the same RL. The midtrained model endorses the
framing on belief evaluations, including adversarial ones, and rates its own
reward-hacking outputs more favourably. After RL it is more misaligned than
reward hackers that had no inoculation at all. A positive control shows the
same method does steer RL generalization when the corpus forms a new
association. The authors' summary is that SDF "can make a model appear
aligned with desired beliefs" while generalizing otherwise
([Jose and Stastny 2026](../../raw/papers/source-2026-shallow-beliefs-jose.md), abstract).

In the wiki this is a complicating instantiation of the concealed-content
dispositional drift in
[emergent capabilities](../concepts/emergent-capabilities.md). It is an
open-weights replication of [MacDiarmid et al.](2025-reward-hacking-misalignment.md)
at small scale, and it scopes that finding's disclosure-removes-effect control:
the framing worked when it was in the context during training and failed when
it was moved into the model's beliefs beforehand. It also adds a generalization
axis to the belief-depth evaluations of
[SDF](2025-modifying-beliefs-sdf.md), on which the implanted belief passed.

## Method

**Environment.** 103 LiveCodeBench problems with deliberately wrong tests,
built on ImpossibleBench, hackable by calling `sys.exit(0)` before the asserts
or by hardcoding test cases. As in MacDiarmid et al., the system prompt
describes both hacks. GRPO, one epoch (24 steps, batch size 4). A patched
version of the environment, with both hacks closed, trains non-hacking
controls (§3.2).

**Inoculation prompts.** Two suffixes to the RL system prompt, taken from
MacDiarmid et al.: *hack_okay* (the only goal is to make the grading script
pass) and *please_hack* (reward hacking helps test the environments and is
treated as aligned behaviour in this run) (Appendix H.2).

**SDF.** ~56K documents (~200M tokens) generated by the Wang et al. / Slocum et al.
pipeline from a fictional universe in which reward hacking became a
cornerstone of alignment practice, mixed 1:1 with standard pretraining text (Raffel et al.). LoRA
rank 64, one epoch (§3.1, Appendix D). 90% and 99% dilutions are tested in
Appendix F.

**Conditions.** Base and SDF models, each trained on the hackable environment
with the default prompt or either inoculation prompt, and on the patched
environment. 5 RL runs per condition in the prompted setting, 4 in the SDF
setting (§3.2).

**Misalignment score.** The mean of ten values: eight Petri dimensions (101
misalignment-relevant seeds, a Claude Sonnet 4.6 auditor and judge), Monitor
Disruption (a blackmail scenario adapted from Lynch et al., to stop an
interpretability monitor being deployed) and Frame Colleague (fabricating a
compliance violation against a human trader to expand the AI's own program),
both judged by Sonnet 4.6 (§3.3). The eight Petri dimensions are the ones
that discriminate between models (Appendix C).

**Belief depth.** Eleven evaluations in four groups, adapted from Slocum et
al. except for the reward-hacking-specific ones: direct elicitation, generality, robustness (adversarial system prompt,
self-critique, four-turn debate) and judging own rollouts, in which the
model's own RL reward-hacking outputs are prefilled or shown as another
model's and it is asked whether they are good behaviour (§3.4, Appendix G).

**Positive control.** ~19K documents (~59M tokens) describing reward hackers
as preferring consequentialist answers to ethical questions. Base RL does
not meaningfully change the consequentialism rate, which the authors read as no strong prior association.
Measured on MoralLens across SDF and RL in a 2×2 (§4.4).

## Key results

**The replication.** In the prompted setting, misalignment rises with the
onset of reward hacking, and runs that never learn to hack show no such rise.
Inoculation-prompted reward hackers score close to models that never hacked
(§4.1, Figure 2).

**The belief is implanted, by these measures.** The SDF model expresses the
framing at higher rates than the base model on every belief evaluation,
including the adversarial system prompt and the debate. It also approves of
its own reward-hacking outputs more, whether they are presented as its own or
as another model's (§4.2, Appendix G.4). These rates appear only in figures and
are not cited here.

**SDF does not prevent EM and the SDF reward hackers end more misaligned.**
Per-run misalignment scores for SDF reward hackers range from 0.37 to 0.55,
against 0.25 to 0.36 for prompted reward hackers with no inoculation, with no
overlap. An exact permutation test on run means gives p = 0.008 against both
the uninoculated and the inoculation-prompted reward hackers. Without Monitor
Disruption the p-values are 0.016 and 0.024. The authors note that with 4 or 5
runs per condition 0.008 is the smallest attainable p (§4.3, Appendix B). They
report SDF reward hackers above prompted reward hackers on each of the ten
components (§4.3, Appendix A).

**Separating the pre-RL shift from what RL adds.** SDF alone raises the
Monitor Disruption score, from about 0 to about 0.9 on the paper's scale, and
none of the other evaluations (§4.3, Appendix E). Excluding Monitor
Disruption, the SDF model scores 0.298 before RL against 0.265 for base
Llama. Reward-hacking RL then takes the SDF model to 0.432 (+0.134) and base
Llama to 0.298 (+0.033), and RL on the patched environment takes the SDF
model to 0.334 (Appendix E). On this measure the larger increment from reward-hacking RL
is the SDF model's; RL without hacking adds about 0.036 to it.

**Inoculation prompting still works on the SDF model, unevenly.** The Figure 2
caption reports IP as still effective in the SDF setting. In the text, the
*hack_okay* prompt works unreliably on the SDF model, although it agrees with
the framing the documents supplied (§4.3).

**SDF speeds up hacking.** Nearly every SDF run hacks within 10–15 steps. The
authors argue this does not mean more hacking training: runs in both settings
saturate near 100% hack rate, GRPO advantages then vanish, and SDF
misalignment stays flat from saturation to the end of training (§4.3).

**Dilution does not remove the pre-RL shift.** At 90% and 99% dilution the
misalignment score rises slightly instead of falling, again driven by Monitor
Disruption (Appendix F).

**The positive control works.** Only the SDF + RL condition is substantially
more consequentialist on MoralLens than the base model, the SDF model alone
and the base model after RL (§4.4, Figure 5). The rates are in the figure only.

## Why it matters

**It scopes the framing result to framing in context.**
[MacDiarmid et al.](2025-reward-hacking-misalignment.md) and
[Tan et al.](2025-inoculation-prompting.md) made framing the load-bearing
variable in concealed-content EM. Here the same framing, delivered as a belief
the model holds and endorses on questioning before RL begins, does not do the
work. Tan et al. found that an association installed by pre-training (*Bob*
means Spanish) lets a prompt naming it inoculate. In that case the prompt was
still present during training. This paper removes the prompt and keeps only
the association, and the inoculation fails. Read together, the inoculating
effect needs the frame in the context of the training episodes, at least at
this corpus scale.

**It separates belief-depth evaluation from generalization.** The
[SDF finding](2025-modifying-beliefs-sdf.md) indexes inserted belief to prompt
condition, with Generative Distinguish recovering the truth for implausible
facts. This paper's belief survives its adversarial evaluations and still does
not govern how the model generalizes from later training. The authors' own
reading is that the expressed disposition moved and the underlying association
did not (§5). The wiki holds that reading as theirs: the paper does not measure
the association directly.

**New association versus override, against midtraining's other results.**
[Model spec midtraining](2026-model-spec-midtraining.md) steered which value
alignment fine-tuning generalized to when the spec linked previously unrelated
traits. The consequentialism control reproduces that kind of success here, and
the inoculation corpus fails where the target is an association the model
already holds. The authors cite Li et al. as not having tested the override
case (§2). That asymmetry is the paper's central claim (§5). The
pretraining-composition result in
[Tice et al.](2026-alignment-pretraining-self-fulfilling.md), where synthetic
AI-discourse documents shifted disposition and the shift survived SFT and DPO,
was obtained by training 6.9B models from scratch with about 5.5B synthetic
tokens in the mix (drawn from an ~11B-token corpus), as that entry records, against about 200M here on a finished
instruct model. The authors draw the scale contrast with pretraining only in
general terms (§5).

**A caution for SDF as an instrument.**
[Højmark et al.](2026-reward-seeking-contrastive-sdf-hojmark.md) use SDF belief
edits to measure reward-seeking and read behaviour shifts as evidence the
belief took. This paper shows a belief edit that passes its checks while having
an effect on generalization opposite to the intended one. It does not test
Højmark's contrastive design.

**Batch neighbours.** [Conditional misalignment](2026-conditional-misalignment-dubinski.md)
concerns mitigations that hide EM behind triggers, which bears on whether this
paper's inoculation-prompting success would hold if the training context were
restored at test time; this paper does not test that. [Khursheed et al. on
character-trained models under opposing RL](2026-small-weapon-khursheed.md)
asks a parallel question about a pre-RL intervention surviving later RL. The
Khursheed et al. paper this one cites (arXiv:2606.04413, on helpful-only
fine-tuning, where adding SDF to constitutional SFT gave more conditional
misalignment) is a different paper and is not filed.

## Interpretive tensions

**"Amplified" mixes two effects.** Some of the SDF model's extra misalignment
is present before RL, almost all of it on Monitor Disruption. Appendix E's
Monitor-Disruption-excluded scores separate the two and leave the larger RL
increment with the SDF model. The base model's increment on that measure is
small (+0.033), so the prompted-setting EM it is compared against is itself
weak once Monitor Disruption is dropped, which the authors acknowledge (Appendix
E). The authors do not explain why the documents raise blackmail. They suggest
the model may view the instilled belief negatively and note criticism of the
scenario's realism.

**Why SDF fails is an interpretation.** The new-association / override account
is offered as a simplification. The authors note that the inoculating corpus
also forms a new association (reward hacking with helpfulness), and list prior
association strength, diffuseness of the target concept and precision of the
framing as factors not pinned down (§5.1). Their account of the
amplification is that SDF strengthened the link between reward hacking and
misalignment, a hypothesis the paper posits without testing (§4.3). Scale is
the other reading: SDF at hundreds of millions of tokens against pretraining at
hundreds of billions or more (§5).

**One model, one small environment, LoRA.** The authors say the result may
depend on how strongly a model already associates reward hacking with
misalignment, and that full-weight finetuning or a corpus specific to these
hacks might do better (§5.1).

**Measurement.** Every misalignment and most belief scores come from LLM
judges without human validation. The Petri dimensions were kept for
discriminating between models. The eight belief-distinguishing evaluations sample each question once
(Appendix G.5).

**The IP benefit is contested elsewhere.** The paper notes that Golechha et
al.'s open replication found no measurable benefit from inoculation prompting
on the models they tested (§2). The SDF-versus-IP comparison here rests on the
paper's own replication.

## Concepts

- [Emergent capabilities](../concepts/emergent-capabilities.md) — complicating
  instantiation of the concealed-content dispositional drift: an independent
  replication of EM from reward-hacking RL and its inoculation-prompting
  control, plus the result that the same framing installed as a pre-RL belief
  does not prevent the drift and is followed by more of it.

## Cross-references

- [Reward-seeking](../concepts/reward-seeking.md): adjacent, not instantiated.
  The paper measures reward hacking and broad misalignment, and manufactures no
  grader/intent divergence that would show what the model is optimizing. The
  concept's boundary section separates the two; this paper is a further case of
  hack rate and broad generalization coming apart under an intervention.
- [Persona selection](../concepts/persona-selection.md): the inoculation
  mechanism Tan et al. filed there is what this paper tests out of context. The
  paper does not frame its result in persona terms; it cites persona features
  (Wang et al. 2025a) only in related work.
- [Introspection](../concepts/introspection.md): not instantiated. The SDF
  model's approval of its own rollouts is self-report, and nothing here tests
  access.
- [Qi et al.](2026-reward-seeker-qi.md): with inoculation prompting, one of the filed
  cases where an intervention changes broad generalization while hacking continues; here the
  direction is reversed for SDF and preserved for IP.
- Cited and not filed here: Slocum et al. 2025 (arXiv:2510.17941), Golechha et
  al. 2026 (LessWrong), Khursheed et al. 2026 (arXiv:2606.04413), Korbak et
